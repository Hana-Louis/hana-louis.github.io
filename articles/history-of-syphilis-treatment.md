## Every Treatment For Syphilis

Do you know about Syphilis? It's this cruel infection that causes not only gruesome pain to a person, but also the feeling of horror. Luckily, in 1928 an antibiotic came to us that ended those nightmares. A drop of this antibiotic would woo this disease bye bye. But years ago, people did not have this antibiotic. Just how on earth did they cope with this horrific disease?

Syphilis is an actively spreading infection worldwide. It is caused by a very very sensitive bacteria known as *Treponima pallidum* that cannot even live on a surface. And humans seem to be its only host.

Once in the contaminated area the bacteria starts multiplying. But not at the rate like those of E.coli, instead it multiplies every 30 hours. The bacteria passes through the extracellular matrix with the help of their abundant lipoprotein and drilling skills, attach to host cells and then scatter along the rest of the body. After the first symptoms, the bacteria can stay dormant for up to 20-30 years, and then jump back alive by affecting every organ and inflecting various symptoms that mimic other diseases. This eventually produces highly destructive lesions in almost any tissue and bone.

During the first outbreak, physicians warned the disease to be incurable. The majority often linked the plague with astrological conjunction and religious theories, often stating that the disease was a consequence of blasphemy and sins. Their way of addressing those who are infected, were of course, a death punishment. 

As time passed by, a fumigation stove was believed to be a fashionable method of treatment, and patients were trapped inside a huge barrel. Mercury was inside the stove and was burned beneath the patient to encourage severe sweating and salivation, which were believed to be the only way for the bacteria to be eliminated. The side effects included degrading teeth health, getting poisoned, and ulcers.

In the 15th century, earlier records showed that a potion extracted from the guaiac tree was used as a syphilis treatment. The reactions of the treatment were similar to the fumigation, but this time with diarrhea and vomit. Though the tree was not at all poisonous or harmful, large amounts of its use was enough to cause discomfort to the body.

In the 16th century, the belief that sweating and salivation were still strong and during this time mercury was becoming an advancing study, the difference is that it was now taken as a pill or by injecting liquid mercury on the genitals. Injections were supposed to be taken at multiple intervals to ensure its effectiveness. Just like the previous treatments it led to intense vomiting, bowel movements, and pneumonia.

Improvements were seen as a less toxic treatment than mercury, Bismuth, was introduced in 1844. This surprisingly had more effect by actually killing the bacterium. Upon injection, Bi3+ is absorbed into the bloodstream that effectively suppresses the enzymatic activity of Tet(X) resistance protein in bacteria. Bismuth drugs also disrupt pathways in the pathogen, damage their defenses, eliminate the bacterial pH-buffering via binding several key enzymes, and contribute to the healing of lesions.

And one of the famously recognized treatments, largely because of its peculiarity, was the involvement of malaria as a treatment. The main reason was to induce a massive fever by injecting the protozoan Plasmodium vivax. Once the fever cooked out the syphilis bacteria, physicians used interval injections of quinine to then treat the malaria. It then got the name as the most effective treatment in the early 1900s, but its value for tertiary stage of syphilis was less apparent. Risks included anemia, liver damage, and the fever and malaria itself.

In 1908, the first scientifically effective treatment for syphilis was arsphenamine (Salvarsan), an acting antibiotic that can bind to the bacterium and kill it without affecting human cells. A safer drug was later made known as Neosalvarsan. Once injected, the body metabolizes the compound into an arsenic form. The active arsenic targets and binds to sulfhydryl (-SH) groups on specific proteins and enzymes of T. pallidum and inhibits their energy and cellular activities, eventually killing the bacteria. However, there were some problems with solubility, and the treatment was not safe for those who are gravely ill. Moreover, side effects included rashes, liver damage, and risks to life and limb.
 
And at last the discovered drug in 1928 was later recognized as the main treatment for syphilis, which was the penicillin. Penicillin has a structure called B-lactum ring, binding with the *T. pallidum* proteins to induce the bacteria from producing their cell walls, eventually killing it. The medicine is usually injected in the buttocks and can last up to a week from just one shot; a steroid tablet or injection is given before the antibiotic is taken to avoid the side effects of chills, fever, headache, and rashes. 

Early syphilis treatment like those of the bismuth drugs, mercury injection, malaria, and salvarsan were barely effective because of the multiple injections that were followed. The treatment must be in the body long enough, that is why the earliest treatments had to be injected usually, while oral ingestion was also an option. 
​So, just how on earth did they cope? The truth is, they barely did. For centuries, surviving syphilis meant choosing between a slow, disfiguring death from the disease, or a brutal, agonizing treatment that might just kill you faster than the bacteria could. It was a dark, desperate era of human experimentation, where injecting liquid mercury or catching malaria were seen as valid options rather than doing nothing. It makes you realize that we don't just live in the modern world; we live in a privileged one, all thanks to a single, accidental discovery of a mold called penicillin.
