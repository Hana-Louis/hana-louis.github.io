# How did we discover poison?
*Published: August 2, 2026 | Author: Hana*

# Content

If there's one thing humans are good at, it's probably being able to discover new things. Give a cave man some stones and sticks and next week it may probably have crafted a spear used for hunting. Welp, that's exactly what happened years ago, and for some reason their gears got upgraded into a deadly poisonous hunting spear. Those Baroque gals would call it witchcraft, but thanks to science, we call it today as poison.

Ah yes, the thing that apothecaries were obsessed with, where chemists were amused with, and the medics found helpful. These professionals were heavily studying poison in the prime of their lifetime, and it was thanks to them that we distinguished it from being witch potion. But, how exactly did we become so close with poison? Knowing it was very dangerous, how then were we able to survive and pass on the knowledge of its existence and capabilities?

Poison, at the time of prehistorical human knowledge, was dominated by animals like snakes and venomous insects. It was not until cave dwellers noticed that there was another source, an unusual source aside from animals. Plants. Plants were known to be harmless, until dwellers saw animals lay dead upon eating particular types of plants. It was not long after that those same dwellers used those plants to their advantage. Tools with such accessories have been found to be six thousands years old, and one of the most popular plants extracted for the upgrading of caveman equipment was found to be the Strychnos toxifera (Loganiaceae) (Nepovimova and Kuca, 2019).

This practice of weaponizing nature inevitably passed down through generations, evolving from a simple way to hunt food into a sophisticated tool for warfare and political power. The Ancient Greeks, for instance, weaponized it so heavily in battle using poison-tipped arrows that their words for bow, "toxon"; and poison, "toxicon", became permanently linked. Meanwhile, across the Mediterranean, the famous Ebers Papyrus of Ancient Egypt revealed that priests and healers were blending toxic plants and minerals into secretive, ritualistic concoctions.
But perhaps the most extreme dedication to the craft came from Ancient China and Pontus, where the legendary Emperor Shen Nung and Mithridate VI tasted over hundreds of herbs firsthand to map out their medicinal and toxic limits (Hayes and Gilbert, 2009). Talk about a real-life MaoMao moment.

Fun fact: After people like Shen Nung and Mithridate VI mapped out what killed and what cured, ruling classes used it for their favourite game of life and death. Unlike traditional fights using loud swords and spears, poison does the job quietly without leaving a single shred of evidence. This immediately changed their dinner time into a thrilling game where everyone was terrified of their own wine. Honestly, even I wouldn’t want to be suspicious of my delicious savory food, and neither did those emperors, so they had to find a way. 

The first official law against poisoning was declared by Lucius Cornelius Sulla in 82 B.C.E. But spoiler alert: the laws didn't stop the crimes. People were still dying left and right, forcing humans to stop relying on rules and start discovering ways to actually defend themselves. For centuries, this meant playing Russian roulette with royal 'taste testers.' But eventually, this desperate need for defense led scientists to discover ways to chemically isolate substances like arsenic in a corpse, officially giving birth to forensic science.

Humans always thought that poisons and medicines were completely separate things. And honestly, in their defense, you wouldn't normally trust something that can kill you. But the 16th-century physician and chemist Paracelsus saw things differently, famously writing: 'All things are poison, and nothing is without poison; only the dose permits something not to be poison.' And he was right. Centuries before him, Mithridates VI had accidentally stumbled onto this exact concept by diluting very very tiny amounts of toxins to build up his immunity (Magowska, 2021). Paracelsus just took that ancient idea, proved it with science, and changed how we looked at poisons forever. And we can see that testimony in our current timeline! 

So, how did we discover poison exactly? Was it curiosity, our desperation for survival, or just our nature of being craftsmen? I think it was a combination of all. Furthermore, the unity of the scattered experimentations across different parts of the globe also led to the fast advancement in understanding how poison was adapted and why it was adapted. Through these, humanity was able to use poison both as a deadly tool and a powerful cure for certain things. 

What the cavemen saw was not just a coincidence, it was a pattern. The moment they recognized it and acted upon it, was the moment that the future of poisons started.
